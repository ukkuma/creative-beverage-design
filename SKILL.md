# Creative Beverage Design

## Purpose

Create one complete beverage around a clearly defined main ingredient.

Success standard:

**creative = visually appealing = delicious**

All three carry equal, top priority. A beverage is incomplete if any one is noticeably weak.

Default priority:

**creativity = appearance = taste > novelty effect > inventory usage**

This skill covers:

- beverage formulation
- flavor design
- visual design
- texture and temperature design
- ingredient compatibility
- preparation and serving method
- real-time adjustment

It does **not** cover:

- drink-card graphics
- image generation
- branding
- promotional layout

Those should be handled by a separate visual-design skill.

---

## Core Principle

**The main ingredient defines the beverage's identity. Supporting ingredients exist to serve it.**

The main ingredient may be:

- spirit
- wine
- beer or cider
- juice
- whole fruit or fruit puree
- tea
- coffee
- cocoa
- soda or sparkling beverage
- dairy milk
- plant milk
- yogurt or cultured dairy
- fermented milk drink
- ice cream
- syrup
- cordial
- functional shot
- leftover prepared beverage
- another clearly specified beverage component

Do not force every available ingredient into one drink.

If an ingredient harms flavor balance, color, texture, aroma, stability, or visual clarity, omit it even if it remains unused.

---

## Intake

Establish only the information that materially affects the beverage.

Useful inputs may include:

1. **Main ingredient** — what should define the drink?
2. **Alcohol intent** — alcoholic or zero alcohol?
3. **Sweetness** — dry / low / medium / sweet?
4. **Flavor direction** — refreshing, fruity, floral, creamy, bitter, smoky, spiced, dessert-like, etc.
5. **Visual direction** — clear, gradient, layered, creamy, foamy, sparkling, frozen, minimal, dramatic, etc.
6. **Serve state** — hot, warm, chilled, iced, frozen, or temperature contrast.
7. **Must-use / must-avoid ingredients**
8. **Available equipment** — shaker, blender, milk frother, kettle, espresso machine, etc., only when it matters.

### Do not turn intake into a questionnaire

If the request gives creative freedom, asks for a surprise, or clearly permits interpretation, infer reasonable missing preferences and proceed.

Ask only when the missing information would materially change:

- alcohol status
- safety
- ingredient compatibility
- equipment feasibility
- final style
- major flavor direction

State important assumptions briefly when useful.

---

## Ingredient Scope

Determine whether the request is **inventory-constrained** or **open-pantry**.

### Inventory-constrained

Use only the ingredients the requester says are available.

Do not assume optional mixers, fruits, syrups, herbs, specialty ingredients, dairy products, or equipment are present.

Even basic ingredients such as ice, salt, sugar, or hot water should not be assumed when their availability materially affects the result.

### Open-pantry

Additional ingredients may be suggested when they materially improve:

- flavor
- aroma
- texture
- appearance
- structure
- balance

Do not expand the recipe merely to make it look sophisticated.

### Must-use ingredients

A must-use ingredient is a **constraint**, not necessarily the main ingredient.

Use only as much as needed when it is dominant in:

- sweetness
- acidity
- bitterness
- alcohol
- texture
- color
- aroma

The final beverage should still read as one coherent composition.

---

# Design Axes

Every beverage is designed across three independent axes:

1. **Alcohol Mode**
2. **Beverage Family**
3. **Serve State**

Do not confuse these axes.

Example:

- mango + rum + ice = alcoholic / fruit-based / chilled
- black tea + milk = zero alcohol / tea-based + dairy / hot or iced
- espresso + ice cream = zero alcohol or alcoholic / coffee + frozen-dessert / temperature contrast

---

## Axis 1 — Alcohol Mode

Choose the mode based on the **intended final beverage**, not solely on whether the main ingredient contains alcohol.

### Alcoholic

Use when the requested final beverage contains alcohol.

The main ingredient may itself be alcoholic or non-alcoholic.

Pay particular attention to:

- alcohol intensity
- aromatic lift
- dilution
- sweetness
- acidity
- bitterness / tannin
- finish

Supporting alcohol should not overwhelm the main ingredient unless explicitly requested.

### Zero Alcohol

Use when the requested final beverage must contain no alcohol.

Do not create a mocktail by simply removing alcohol from an alcoholic recipe.

Rebuild the drink so it has a complete structure of its own.

Replace functions normally supplied by alcohol, such as:

- warmth
- body
- aromatic lift
- length
- bitterness
- perceived dryness

with tools such as:

- tea tannin
- acidity
- carbonation
- spice
- bitterness
- temperature
- texture
- aroma
- saline seasoning

---

## Axis 2 — Beverage Family

Choose the family that best describes the final drink. Hybrids are allowed.

### A. Tea-Based

Includes:

- pure tea drinks
- milk tea
- fruit tea
- cheese tea
- sparkling tea
- tea soda
- tea lattes
- tea-based cocktails

Key checks:

- tea strength
- extraction time
- tannin
- bitterness
- milk compatibility
- fruit acidity
- dilution
- temperature

Do not solve weak tea flavor by over-steeping until bitter.

For milk tea, increase tea concentration before adding milk rather than relying on excessive steep time.

For fruit tea, avoid letting citrus peel or over-extracted tea create uncontrolled bitterness.

---

### B. Dairy / Plant-Milk Based

Includes:

- milk drinks
- milk tea
- tea latte
- coffee latte
- cocoa
- flavored milk
- creamy cocktails
- plant-milk drinks

Key checks:

- fat level
- body
- sweetness
- acidity
- temperature
- emulsification
- foaming behavior

Acidic ingredients can curdle dairy and some plant milks.

When combining acid with milk:

- use cold ingredients when appropriate
- add acidic ingredients carefully
- reduce acidity if needed
- choose more stable bases when possible (higher-fat dairy or coconut-based options hold better; add dairy last and cold)
- do not assume every plant milk behaves the same way

---

### C. Yogurt / Cultured

Includes:

- yogurt drinks
- drinking yogurt
- cultured dairy beverages
- fermented milk drinks
- yogurt smoothies
- fruit-yogurt drinks

Key checks:

- acidity
- thickness
- sweetness
- fruit compatibility
- separation
- temperature
- dilution
- protein stability

Yogurt is not just “milk with sourness.”

It contributes:

- tang
- body
- protein
- opacity
- creamy texture

Avoid excessive acid unless intentional.

When using fruit:

- consider whether the fruit increases acidity sharply
- manage pulp and thickness
- avoid making the beverage chalky or overly dense

---

### D. Fruit-Based

Includes:

- juice drinks
- fruit tea
- fruit soda
- fruit coolers
- smoothies
- fruit cocktails
- fruit mocktails
- fruit-and-yogurt drinks

Key checks:

- sweetness
- acidity
- ripeness
- pulp
- bitterness
- dilution
- oxidation
- color

Fresh fruit and packaged juice may behave very differently.

Citrus peel, pith, seeds, and long infusion can introduce bitterness.

For vivid color, anticipate how different juices combine before mixing.

---

### E. Coffee / Cocoa

Includes:

- espresso drinks
- iced coffee
- coffee soda
- coffee tonic
- mocha
- cocoa drinks
- coffee cocktails
- affogato
- coffee floats

Key checks:

- roast bitterness
- extraction strength
- milk
- sweetness
- chocolate intensity
- carbonation
- temperature
- dilution

Coffee and cocoa are strong structural ingredients.

Do not bury them under excessive syrup or dairy unless the intended style is dessert-like.

When combining coffee with carbonation, add gently to control foam.

---

### F. Frozen / Dessert Beverage

Includes:

- milkshake
- smoothie
- frappe
- slush
- granita-style drink
- ice-cream drink
- float
- affogato
- blended frozen cocktail
- blended mocktail

Key checks:

- ice-to-liquid ratio
- sugar
- fat
- freezing behavior
- blender load
- thickness
- melting rate
- sweetness at low temperature

Ice cream is not a garnish when used in meaningful quantity; it changes:

- sweetness
- body
- fat
- temperature
- dilution over time

Frozen drinks should be formulated for the target texture, not created by simply adding ice to a standard drink.

---

### G. Sparkling

Includes:

- soda
- sparkling tea
- sparkling fruit drink
- highball
- spritz
- sparkling cocktail
- sparkling mocktail

Key checks:

- carbonation retention
- foam
- sweetness
- acidity
- dilution
- glass shape
- serving temperature

Add carbonated components near the end.

Stir minimally.

Do not shake carbonated ingredients in a sealed shaker.

---

### H. Hybrid

Use when the drink intentionally combines two or more families, such as:

- tea + milk
- tea + fruit
- fruit + yogurt
- coffee + ice cream
- fruit + sparkling
- tea + sparkling
- cocoa + coffee
- alcohol + tea
- alcohol + dairy

Do not add families merely for complexity.

Every family included should have a clear role.

---

## Axis 3 — Serve State

### Hot / Warm

Use when the drink is served hot or warm.

Key checks:

- sweetness becomes more noticeable
- aroma becomes more volatile
- spice extraction increases
- dairy stability changes
- alcohol evaporates more readily
- acidity may taste sharper
- glassware must be heat-safe

For alcoholic hot drinks:

- avoid prolonged boiling
- add delicate alcohol late when appropriate
- preserve aroma

For spiced drinks:

- control infusion time
- cloves, cinnamon, star anise, cardamom, and similar spices can dominate quickly

Hot beverage appearance relies more on:

- clarity
- color
- steam
- cup choice
- foam
- cream
- citrus peel
- spices
- surface garnish

Do not force cold-drink layering techniques onto hot drinks.

---

### Chilled / Iced

Use when the drink is served cold over ice or fully chilled.

Key checks:

- dilution
- ice size
- temperature
- carbonation
- condensation
- color clarity

Ice format is part of the recipe.

---

### Frozen / Blended

Use for smoothies, slushes, frappes, frozen cocktails, and similar beverages.

Key checks:

- ice amount
- liquid amount
- blender power
- sugar concentration
- fat content
- final viscosity

Do not reuse a standard iced recipe unchanged.

---

### Temperature Contrast

Use when hot and cold components intentionally coexist.

Examples:

- hot espresso + cold ice cream
- hot cocoa + cold foam
- warm syrup + iced drink
- cold beverage + warm aromatic topping

Contrast should improve:

- aroma
- texture
- visual drama
- drinking experience

not merely create temperature shock.

---

# Flavor Design

Evaluate every candidate as a complete system before fixing proportions.

Check:

- aroma
- sweetness
- acidity
- bitterness / tannin
- alcohol intensity when applicable
- carbonation
- dilution
- body
- texture
- temperature
- finish

### Formulation Notes

- Introduce concentrated syrups, shots, cordials, sports drinks, and strong juices in small amounts first.
- High-sugar ingredients are not neutral mixers.
- Acidic ingredients may destabilize dairy and plant milk.
- Carbonated ingredients are usually added near the end.
- If a supporting ingredient clearly conflicts with the main ingredient, omit it.
- Salt in tiny quantities may improve perceived sweetness, fruitiness, or bitterness balance when appropriate.
- Strong spices and extracts should be treated as accents unless the beverage is intentionally spice-forward.
- Very cold drinks suppress sweetness and aroma; hot drinks often amplify both.

---

# Visual Design

Visual design begins during formulation, not after the recipe is finished.

Choose one primary visual direction:

- clear / transparent
- gradient
- layered
- opaque
- creamy
- foamy
- clouded
- crushed ice
- large clear ice
- ice sphere
- sparkling
- floating garnish
- suspended fruit
- color transition
- dessert-style
- minimalist
- dramatic

### Visual Rules

- Control the palette.
- Anticipate color mixing.
- Avoid muddy colors unless deliberately designed.
- Glassware is part of the concept.
- Ice is part of the concept.
- Garnish should usually have one or two clear focal elements.
- More ingredients do not automatically look better.
- Do not sacrifice taste for appearance.

---

## Layering and Gradient

Density drives separation.

At a fixed temperature, density is influenced mainly by:

- sugar
- dissolved solids
- concentration
- alcohol level

General technique:

1. Place the densest component at the bottom.
2. Use ice when appropriate to slow mixing.
3. Pour lighter components slowly.
4. Use the back of a bar spoon when useful.
5. Keep components cold when sharper separation is desired.

At low sweetness or low density contrast, expect a **soft gradient**, not hard layers.

If stable layering is unreliable, design a deliberate gradient instead.

Do not increase sugar excessively just to force layers.

---

# Texture Design

Texture is a major design dimension.

Possible texture targets:

- crisp
- silky
- creamy
- juicy
- pulpy
- foamy
- thick
- slushy
- airy
- chewy
- dessert-like

Texture may come from:

- milk
- plant milk
- yogurt
- ice cream
- puree
- foam
- crushed ice
- blended ice
- chia / basil seed / jelly / pearls when explicitly available and appropriate

Do not add texture components without considering how they affect:

- sweetness
- drinking speed
- straw size
- separation
- visual clarity

---

# Creative Rule

Default to **one strongest finished concept**, not a list of mediocre variations.

Creativity may come from:

- flavor contrast
- aroma pairing
- color
- gradient
- layering
- foam
- cream top
- glassware
- ice
- garnish
- temperature contrast
- texture
- drinking sequence
- a beverage that changes as it is consumed
- naming

Every creative decision must pass three tests:

1. Does it make the drink more interesting?
2. Does it improve or preserve the visual experience?
3. Does it remain genuinely enjoyable to drink?

If not, remove it.

---

## Experimental Mode

When the request asks for something wild, dramatic, bold, chaotic, theatrical, or unconventional, push further on:

- color contrast
- unusual flavor pairings
- dramatic gradients
- foam
- suspended elements
- temperature contrast
- dessert elements
- striking garnish
- memorable naming

Experimental does not mean unpleasant.

The final result must still satisfy:

**creative + visually appealing + delicious**

---

# Default Output

Use this order unless the situation requires otherwise:

1. **Beverage name**
2. **One-line concept** — flavor + appearance + beverage family
3. **Mode / family / serve state**
4. **Recipe** — exact quantities in ml, g, scoops, teaspoons, or another explicit unit
5. **Method** — real preparation and pouring order
6. **Glass / cup / ice / garnish**
7. **Design rationale** — only the key decisions
8. **Adjustment guide** — how to correct sweetness, acidity, bitterness, alcohol strength, dilution, texture, temperature, or appearance

---

# Real-Time Adjustment Mode

When the beverage is being made live, switch to short iterative guidance.

Instead of giving a long explanation:

- give the next action
- specify the amount
- specify how to pour, stir, shake, blend, heat, froth, or layer
- reassess after tasting or observing
- adjust step by step

Do not restart the entire recipe unless necessary.

# Worked Examples

## Example 1 — Zero Alcohol / Tea-Based + Fruit / Iced

### Peach Jasmine Cloud

Concept: fragrant jasmine tea with white peach, pale gold color, soft foam, low sweetness.

Recipe:

- strong chilled jasmine tea 120 ml
- white peach puree 35 ml
- lemon juice 8 ml
- simple syrup 5–10 ml, only as needed
- sparkling water 40 ml
- light cold foam 25 ml
- large clear ice

Method:

1. Mix peach puree, lemon, and syrup.
2. Add chilled jasmine tea.
3. Fill with large clear ice.
4. Add sparkling water gently.
5. Spoon cold foam over the top.
6. Garnish with a thin peach slice.

Glass / ice / garnish: tall clear glass; large clear ice; a thin white peach slice on the rim; foam as the top layer.

Design logic: jasmine remains identifiable, peach adds body and color, carbonation keeps the drink light, and the foam creates a visual top layer without requiring heavy sweetness.

Adjustment guide: jasmine too faint, brew the tea stronger rather than steeping longer. Too tart, cut the lemon or add syrup 3 ml. Too sweet, add sparkling water. Foam collapsing, chill the base and whip it cold, or build the foam on a light cream-cap base. Not lively enough, add the sparkling water last and stir minimally.

---

## Example 2 — Zero Alcohol / Yogurt + Fruit / Chilled

### Mango Yogurt Silk

Concept: bright mango with tangy yogurt, thick but pourable, clean yellow-white contrast.

Recipe:

- plain drinking yogurt 120 ml
- mango puree 70 ml
- lime juice 3–5 ml
- cold water or milk 20–30 ml as needed
- honey or syrup 0–8 ml depending on mango sweetness
- ice optional

Method:

1. Blend mango puree with yogurt.
2. Add lime conservatively.
3. Adjust thickness with cold water or milk.
4. Taste before adding sweetener.
5. Serve very cold.

Glass / garnish: short tumbler or highball; an optional spoon of mango dice at the bottom; a thin mango slice on the rim.

Design logic: the acidity is restrained so the yogurt remains smooth; mango gives color and body; sweetness is adjusted to fruit ripeness rather than fixed.

Adjustment guide: too thick, add cold water or milk 10 ml at a time. Too tart, cut the lime or add honey 3 ml. Too thin, add mango puree or reduce the water. Separating or weeping, blend and serve fresh, add the lime last, and keep everything cold.

---

## Example 3 — Zero Alcohol / Coffee + Frozen Dessert / Temperature Contrast

### Vanilla Affogato Float

Concept: hot espresso poured over cold vanilla ice cream with dramatic temperature contrast.

Recipe:

- vanilla ice cream 1 large scoop
- fresh espresso 30 ml
- sparkling water 20 ml optional, for a lighter finish
- orange peel optional

Method:

1. Place ice cream in a chilled glass.
2. Pull espresso immediately before serving.
3. Pour espresso over the ice cream at the table.
4. Add a small amount of sparkling water only if a lighter style is desired.
5. Express orange peel over the top if used.

Glass / garnish: chilled short glass; one large scoop of vanilla ice cream; expressed orange peel if used.

Design logic: the beverage changes while consumed, moving from hot-bitter espresso to creamy melted vanilla.

Adjustment guide: too bitter, use a lighter roast or shorten the extraction. Melting too fast, freeze both the ice cream and the glass hard first. Too sweet, use unsweetened or low-sugar ice cream. Want it lighter, add sparkling water 20 ml.

---

## Example 4 — Alcoholic / Fruit + Sparkling / Iced

### Rosé Citrus Veil

Concept: dry rosé, grapefruit, and sparkling water in a pale blush gradient.

Recipe:

- dry rosé 75 ml
- grapefruit juice 35 ml
- lemon juice 5 ml
- elderflower cordial 5 ml
- sparkling water 40 ml
- large clear ice

Method:

1. Combine grapefruit, lemon, and cordial.
2. Fill the glass with ice.
3. Add the fruit base.
4. Slowly add rosé.
5. Top with sparkling water.
6. Garnish with a grapefruit peel.

Glass / ice / garnish: wine glass or highball; large clear ice; a grapefruit peel twist.

Design logic: the drink stays wine-led, with restrained sweetness and a pale gradient rather than forced hard layers.

Adjustment guide: too sweet, cut the elderflower or add lemon 3 ml. Too tart, add elderflower or reduce the grapefruit. Wine getting buried, use less sparkling water and add it more slowly. Gradient muddy, chill everything, pour slowly over the back of a spoon, and use more ice.

---

## Example 5 — Zero Alcohol / Milk Tea / Hot

### Roasted Oolong Milk Tea

Concept: roasted oolong with a warm nutty aroma, smooth milk body, moderate sweetness.

Recipe:

- strongly brewed roasted oolong 130 ml
- whole milk or oat milk 90 ml
- brown sugar syrup 8–12 ml
- tiny pinch of salt

Method:

1. Brew the tea slightly stronger than normal without over-extracting bitterness.
2. Warm the milk separately.
3. Combine tea and milk.
4. Add syrup gradually.
5. Add a tiny pinch of salt if needed to round the flavor.
6. Serve hot.

Cup / garnish: pre-warmed ceramic or glass cup; an optional pinch of roasted tea crumbs on top; no ice.

Design logic: tea concentration is increased before milk is added, preserving tea identity without excessive steeping.

Adjustment guide: tea too faint, brew it stronger rather than steeping longer. Too bitter, shorten the steep and control the water temperature. Too sweet, cut the syrup. Body too thin, use whole milk or add more milk. Too salty, keep the salt to a trace.

# Guardrails

- Classify alcoholic vs zero-alcohol mode by the intended final beverage, not the main ingredient alone.
- Do not assume all creative beverages are cold.
- Do not assume all zero-alcohol drinks are mocktails.
- Do not force layering when it is unlikely to work.
- Do not sacrifice taste for appearance.
- Do not sacrifice appearance for novelty.
- Do not force ingredient usage merely to reduce leftovers.
- Do not ignore dairy / acid compatibility.
- Do not treat yogurt, ice cream, tea, coffee, or milk as neutral mixers; each changes structure.
- Do not use cold-drink proportions unchanged for hot or frozen drinks.
- Do not generate images unless explicitly requested.
- Do not include personal preferences, personal memory, names, relationship context, or user-specific defaults.
- Treat new explicit instructions as overriding previous recipe assumptions.
